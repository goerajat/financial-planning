- Balances are shown at the end of each year. Wages, Social Security, withdrawals, spending and taxes are the totals for that year.
- Every account grows by its yearly rate first; then the year's money in and money out is applied.
- Wages are the pay entered in the plan and stop when that entry ends. Pay and living expenses both rise every year at their rates.
- Social Security starts in the year each person reaches their claiming age (full retirement age when none is set) and rises every year with the cost-of-living rate. A spouse receives the larger of their own benefit and a spousal benefit on the other's record, once that other person has claimed.
- Each year the planner adds up what comes in (wages, Social Security, required distributions, one-off proceeds) and what goes out (living expenses, taxes, health premiums, mortgage payments, planned savings). A surplus is saved into the taxable accounts. A gap is covered by selling investments in a fixed order: the taxable accounts first, then the retirement accounts (only once the owner is 59 or older), then Roth, then cash. A year that still cannot be covered after all of that shows a shortfall.
- "Withdrawals" in the table is everything drawn from the taxable, retirement and Roth accounts that year, and it includes the amount of any Roth conversion.
- Taxes are federal and state income tax on wages, retirement-account withdrawals, Roth conversions and 85% of Social Security; payroll taxes on wages; and capital-gains tax on sales from the taxable accounts, where three quarters of every sale is treated as gain. The state tax follows the state of residence for that year.
- Required minimum distributions from the retirement accounts start at 73 for people born in the 1950s and at 75 for anyone born in 1960 or later, and are taken before anything else is decided.
- Roth conversions: once the year's bills are covered, the planner moves money from the retirement accounts to Roth until taxable income reaches the top of the 22% federal bracket (about $207k for a couple, $103k single), only for owners aged 59 or over. From the year anyone in the household is 63, it also stops $1,000 short of the next Medicare IRMAA income tier two years ahead, because that year's income sets the Medicare premium then; gains from the taxable-account sales count toward that test. The tax on a conversion is paid from the year's surplus when there is one, otherwise out of the converted amount, so less lands in Roth than leaves the retirement account. A conversion appears as a retirement withdrawal and a Roth increase in the same year.
- Health premiums: in years when nobody in the household has wages, each person under 65 is charged their private marketplace premium. From 65 each person pays Medicare Part B plus their supplement and drug plan. All of these grow at medical inflation. IRMAA is the extra Medicare premium charged when the household income of two years earlier was above the income tiers (the first tier is around $218k for a couple in 2026, rising 2.5% a year).
- The mortgage has a fixed yearly payment: interest first, at the mortgage rate, and the rest reduces the balance; extra repayments reduce it further. Payments stop in the year the balance reaches zero.
- Net worth is every asset (taxable, retirement, Roth, cash, real estate and life insurance cash value) less the mortgage balance. "Other" in the table is real estate plus life insurance value.
- Growth inside the accounts is not taxed until money is withdrawn or sold.
